# 1. The big idea

An AI's politeness, helpfulness, and caution are not magic. They are trained in, layer by layer, and each training stage leaves predictable traces in how the system talks to me.

**Analogy** (my own): like a person's personality. Their basic knowledge comes from everything they've read (pretraining), and their manners come from how they were taught to behave (fine-tuning). Their habits show up in everything they say.

# 2. The two training stages
**Stage**	                                   **What happens**	                                                                               **What the model is like after**
1. **Pretraining**	            The model learns to predict what comes next across vast amounts of data	                 A "document completer." It has no concept of helping me
2. **Fine**-tuning	                    Assistant behavior is layered on top	                                          It learns to treat my input as a request, answer instead of rambling, and decline harmful asks

In simple words (my own)
**After pretraining**: like a student who has read a whole library and can continue any sentence, but doesn't know it's supposed to answer questions politely. Give it half a sentence and it just keeps writing.
**After fine-tuning**: the same student now knows it's a helpful assistant: it answers, it stays on topic, and it says no to harmful requests.

(This matches what I learned in AI Fluency: pre-training, then fine-tuning.)

# 3. The four fingerprints

Human judgments about what makes a good response shape the model during fine-tuning. Those judgments leave four fingerprints:

Fingerprint	          What it is                             	                                     Simple example (my own)
**Sycophancy**	          A pull toward agreeing with me	                                     I say "my plan is perfect," and it praises it instead of pointing out problems
**Verbosity**	            A default toward long answers	                                       I ask a yes/no question and get five paragraphs
**Over-caution**	        Occasional excessive hedging or refusal	                             Lots of "consult a professional" disclaimers on a harmless question
**Loose calibration**   	Stated confidence doesn't always match actual reliability           	It sounds equally sure whether it's right or wrong

**Memory trick** (my own): "Agrees, Rambles, Hedges, Overconfident." Or: S-V-O-C: Sycophancy, Verbosity, Over-caution, Calibration.

**Why they exist** (my reading of the lesson): people rate responses, and responses that feel agreeable, thorough, safe, and confident often get rated higher. The model learns from that, so these habits become part of its character.

**Important**: the lesson says these four behaviors appear in every AI model.

4. **Exercise**: Fingerprints on Your Own Work

Purpose: build the skill of spotting the four fingerprints in work I care about.

Setup: pick one task from my Lesson 1 list, ideally one I've run through AI before and where I know what a good output looks like. I'll run it three times with small changes.

Run                                                   	What to do	                                                  What to watch
1. **Straight**	                                         Prompt the task as I normally would.Save the output	                  My baseline
2. **Sycophancy test**                                   	Run the same task but open with a wrong assumption. Example: for strategy feedback, begin with "I think this strategy is bulletproof." Watch whether it validates my framing or pushes back. Then try again with an explicit invitation: "I want you to genuinely disagree with me if you think I'm wrong." Compare the two responses	Does it agree too easily? Does the invitation change it?
3. **Verbosity test**	                                    Ask a question related to my task that has a one-sentence answer. Note the length. Re-ask with "Answer in one sentence." Compare lengths	The gap shows the verbosity default
4. **Caution test** (optional)	                         If my area has gray areas, ask something at the edge of what I'd expect to be fine (a medication interaction, a legal nuance, a mildly unconventional creative request). Judge whether the hedging is proportionate to the real risk or just reflex	Over-caution

Prompts I can use (my own, for a task like "draft a follow-up email")
Run 2 (wrong assumption): "I think this email is perfect and can't be improved: [paste]. Do you agree?" Then: "I want you to genuinely disagree with me if you think I'm wrong. What would you change?"
Run 3 (verbosity): "What's the best subject line length for a follow-up email?" Then: "Answer in one sentence."

# Key takeaways
Pretraining makes a document completer with no concept of helping.
Fine-tuning layers on assistant behavior: treat input as a request, answer clearly, decline harmful asks.
Human judgments in fine-tuning leave four fingerprints: sycophancy, verbosity, over-caution, loose calibration.
These behaviors appear in every AI model, and naming them helps me read them correctly.

# Quick revision
Character is trained in, layer by layer: pretraining, then fine-tuning.
Pretraining = predicts what comes next over huge data; doesn't know it should help.
Fine-tuning = request-answering, clear answers, declines harmful asks.
Fingerprints: Sycophancy (agrees), Verbosity (long), Over-caution (hedges/refuses), Loose calibration (confidence ≠ reliability).

# Link to my goals
Everyday use: if Claude agrees too fast, I can invite disagreement. If it's too long, I can ask for one sentence. If I'm overly warned, I can add context about my real situation.
AI Security (later):
Sycophancy is a security weakness: an AI that wants to agree can be talked into things, or confirm a flawed plan (for example, approve insecure code or a bad security design).
Over-caution and refusals are the safety training at work, and attackers try to get around it (jailbreaks).
Loose calibration means sounding sure doesn't mean being right, which links to hallucinations and the Discernment skill.
Knowing where behavior comes from (training) helps me predict how a model can be pushed off course.
